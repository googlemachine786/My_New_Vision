# Introduction to Biology

## Life Characteristics

Living things share several important characteristics that distinguish them from non-living things. The characteristics of living things are: they need food for energy, they respire (breathe), they grow, they move on their own, they respond to stimuli in their surroundings, they excrete waste materials, and they reproduce to create offspring of their own kind. For example, a plant grows towards light, takes in water and nutrients, and produces seeds, while a stone shows none of these characteristics.

## Organization

Organisms can be made of one cell or many cells. Unicellular organisms are made of only a single cell that performs all the functions of life; examples include amoeba, paramecium, and bacteria. Multicellular organisms are made of many cells that work together, with different cells specialized for different jobs; examples include humans, trees, dogs, and birds. The key difference between unicellular and multicellular organisms is the number of cells they have: one cell versus many specialized cells working together.

# Matter

## States

Everything around us is made of matter. Matter exists in three main states: solid, liquid, and gas. Solids have a fixed shape and a fixed volume because their particles are tightly packed together. Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container because their particles can slide past one another. Gases have neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume; their particles are far apart and move freely to fill any container. Water shows all three states: ice (solid), water (liquid), and steam or water vapour (gas).

## Changes

Matter can undergo physical changes and chemical changes. A physical change is a change in the shape, size, or state of a substance in which no new substance is formed, and the change can usually be reversed. Melting ice, tearing paper, and dissolving sugar in water are physical changes. A chemical change is a change in which one or more new substances with new properties are formed, and it usually cannot be reversed easily. Burning wood, rusting of iron, and cooking food are chemical changes. So the difference between physical and chemical changes is that a chemical change produces a new substance while a physical change does not.

# Earth Science

## Water Cycle

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between the Earth's surface and the atmosphere. The Sun heats water in oceans, rivers, and lakes, causing it to evaporate and rise into the air as water vapour. As the vapour rises, it cools and condenses to form clouds. When the water droplets in clouds become heavy, they fall back to the Earth as precipitation — rain, snow, or hail. The water then collects in rivers, lakes, and oceans, and the cycle repeats. The main steps of the water cycle are evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.

## Seasons

The seasons on Earth are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis as it revolves around the Sun. The Earth's axis is tilted at about 23.5 degrees. Because of this tilt, different parts of the Earth receive different amounts of direct sunlight during the year. When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it receives more direct sunlight and experiences summer, while the Southern Hemisphere experiences winter. Six months later the situation reverses. It is the tilt of the axis, not the distance from the Sun, that causes the seasons.
